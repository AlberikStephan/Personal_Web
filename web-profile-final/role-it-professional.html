Role

IT Professional

Technology that quietly works in the background.

I work in IT at a 5-star hotel, where reliability matters as much as speed. From networks and devices to everyday support, my job is to make technology feel invisible to guests and staff.

Working in hotel IT taught me quickly that technology only counts when people stop noticing it. My days start with a simple question: is everything running the way it should be? From there, I move between the front desk, the back office, and the guest floors, making sure the tools each team depends on are ready before anyone has to ask. One of the projects I'm proudest of is an AI agent that works as a quiet assistant for the admin team. It handles routine administrative tasks, sends reminders so deadlines and follow-ups don't slip through, and helps with inventory management so supplies are counted and restocked on time. Before this, a lot of that work was done by hand, and small things were easy to forget during a busy shift. Now the team spends less time chasing details and more time on the guests in front of them. I didn't build it to replace anyone. I built it because good tools should give people their time back, and in a hotel, time spent on people is the whole point of the job.

Behind every comfortable stay is a network that rarely gets the credit it deserves. Keeping the connection stable is a big part of my responsibility, because guests expect Wi-Fi to work the moment they walk into their room, and staff rely on it for everything from reservations to payments. I monitor the network, check access points, and look for weak spots before they turn into complaints. When something does go wrong, I would rather find the real cause than apply a quick patch that comes back the next day. I also support the back office, where the work is less visible but just as important. Printers, workstations, shared systems, and accounts all need to behave so the team can finish reports, process documents, and close the day without delays. Then there are guest requests, which can arrive at any time: a TV that won't connect, a device that can't join the network, a meeting room that must be ready in ten minutes. Each one is small on its own, but together they shape how guests feel about the hotel.

What I enjoy most is the human side of the job. Technical problems are rarely the hard part. Explaining them calmly to someone who is tired, in a hurry, or simply frustrated is where the real skill is. When a guest runs into trouble, I avoid jargon and say plainly what is happening, what I am doing about it, and roughly how long it will take. Nobody needs to hear about routers or authentication errors when they just want to join a video call. A clear, friendly explanation often does as much to fix the situation as the repair itself. I also leave short notes about what I found and how I solved it, so the next person on shift doesn't start from zero. That habit has made our team stronger and our response times faster. At the end of the day, I measure a good shift by how little drama it had. If guests enjoyed their stay, the staff worked smoothly, and nobody had to think about the technology, then I did my job well.
